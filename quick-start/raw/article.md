# Three rounds and done: how a small studio runs design feedback

By Bryony Castellow, Lanternfield Journal, 2026-10-20

Every small studio has a story about the project that would not end. Ours was a garden centre site that went through eleven rounds of homepage changes, each one smaller than the last, until nobody could remember what the first version looked like. We were never short of feedback. We were short of a way to finish.

These days we run every design on three rounds of feedback, and we tell the client so on the first call. Knowing there is an end makes people say the important things early.

The first round is for the big picture. We share rough layouts, often grey boxes with real headings, and ask only two questions: does this page do its job, and is anything missing? Clients are kind to a sketch. They tell you what they really think, because it doesn't look finished enough to hurt anyone's feelings.

The second round is for the real thing. Colours, type, photos and words arrive together, so the client sees the page the way a visitor will. This is where most of the useful comments come in, and where we ask the client to gather everyone who has a say. One set of notes from the whole team beats five emails that disagree with each other.

The third round is for polish: a button label, a spacing fix, a photo swap. If something big comes up at this stage, we treat it as new work rather than squeezing it in, and we say so kindly and early.

A few habits make the rounds work. We always say who on our side will act on the feedback, so comments go to a person and not an inbox. We write back what we heard before we change anything, as a short list the client can tick. And we keep a running note of the decisions, so nobody has to remember why the menu moved.

None of this is clever. Feedback is a conversation with an ending, and both sides relax once they can see it. The kindest thing a client ever told us came in a third round: it was the first project where they always knew what was happening next.

If you take one thing from this, make it the first round. Show something rough, ask simple questions and listen. The earlier the hard feedback arrives, the cheaper it is to act on.
